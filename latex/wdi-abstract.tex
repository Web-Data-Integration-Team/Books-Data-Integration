\begin{abstract}
We integrate book data from Goodreads (52,478 records), DBpedia (59,270) and
Amazon (40,000) into a common schema. Following the feedback, the entity is a
book \emph{edition} (e.g.\ the 2018 Putnam hardcover of \emph{Where the Crawdads
Sing}), not a work. Using the ISBN only as an evaluation key, 6,087 editions
occur in at least two datasets. Many works appear in several editions with the
same title and author (6,588 works with 33,540 editions in the Amazon subset),
which makes telling editions apart the central challenge for identity
resolution. The integrated schema has 14 attributes, 12 of which occur in at
least two datasets; \texttt{authors} and \texttt{genres} are list attributes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Use Case and Entity Definition}

The goal is to integrate book information from three independently maintained
sources that describe overlapping books with different attribute names,
formats and levels of detail. One record of the integrated dataset describes
one \textbf{edition}: a specific publication of a work by a publisher, in a
format (hardcover, paperback, \dots), at a date, with its own page count and ISBN.
Goodreads and Amazon list editions directly. A DBpedia resource describes a
work, but its infobox values (ISBN, publisher, release date, pages) are those
of the first edition, so each DBpedia record is treated as the first edition of
its work. Matching therefore has to distinguish, e.g., the hardcover, paperback,
large-print and movie tie-in editions of the same novel, which share title and
author but differ in publisher, date, format and page count.

\section{Datasets}

\textbf{Goodreads.} The ``Best Books Ever'' dataset (scraped from the Goodreads
list of the same name) lists one edition per book. We use the original CSV;
an earlier copy had been re-saved with a spreadsheet program, which had turned
91\% of the ISBNs into scientific notation and altered dates.

\textbf{DBpedia.} All 59,270 resources of type \texttt{dbo:Book} were
downloaded from the public SPARQL endpoint. Because the endpoint silently cuts
off long-running queries, the download (1) lists the book URIs in partitions by
first character, paged with \texttt{LIMIT 10000 OFFSET} (below the 40,000 offset
limit), and (2) fetches the attributes for 150 known URIs per query
(\texttt{VALUES}); a block that returns a partial result (HTTP 206) is split
and fetched again. The final count equals \texttt{COUNT(dbo:Book)}. Year and
language combine \texttt{dbo:releaseDate}/\texttt{dbo:language} with the raw
infobox properties \texttt{dbp:releaseDate}, \texttt{dbp:pubDate} and
\texttt{dbp:language}, which lowers missing values from 88.5\% to 11.4\%
(year) and from 87.6\% to 20.4\% (language). Unlike the first version, books
without an ISBN are no longer excluded, so 39.0\% of the records have no ISBN.

\textbf{Amazon.} The metadata file of Amazon Reviews 2023 (McAuley Lab)
contains 4,448,181 book products. 4,336,308 have a title and an author, and
3,526,297 of these also have an ISBN; this is the population we sample from.
Products without ISBN (mostly Kindle and Audible) are excluded because the
ISBN is our gold-standard key. From this population we take the
\textbf{40,000 editions with the most ratings} (at least 6,506 ratings each).
The selection uses only Amazon's own rating counts, no information from the
other datasets. We chose popularity because Goodreads ``Best Books Ever'' is
itself a popularity-based list: a uniform random sample of 40,000 editions
(fixed seed) shares only 331 editions with Goodreads, compared with 2,169 for
the popularity-based subset (Table~\ref{tab:overlap}). Amazon reports one
rating count for all editions of a work, so the selection contains popular
works together with many of their editions -- exactly the hard cases for
edition-level matching. This choice has a cost. The overlap figures in Table 3 are higher than they would be for a random Amazon sample. 
They describe this subset and should not be generalized to all of Amazon. 
Our aim is a benchmark with enough shared editions, not a representative sample.

sources use different genre vocabularies.

\section{Overlap Estimation}

Two records describe the same edition if they share an ISBN (ISBN-10 and
ISBN-13 normalised to ISBN-13). The ISBN is used only for this estimate and as
the gold standard; the matchers in Phase~II will not use it. Since records
without an ISBN cannot be counted, the numbers in Table~\ref{tab:overlap} are
lower bounds.

With 6,087 editions in at least two datasets, the requirement of 1,000 is met
even under this strict criterion. The total number of distinct editions is
well above 2,500, since the three sources contain 151,748 records with at most
6,087 shared editions.

To show the difficulty of edition-level matching, we also compared works
(normalised title and first-author surname). Goodreads and DBpedia share
10,619 works; for 4,759 of the 7,561 shared works with ISBNs on both sides
(63\%), the two sources list \emph{different} editions. Amazon and Goodreads
share 3,146 works (22\% in different editions), and within the Amazon subset
6,588 works appear in more than one edition (33,540 records). A matcher based
on title and author alone would therefore produce many false matches; date,
publisher, format and page count are needed to tell editions apart.
In Phase II, the matchers will therefore compare title, authors, publisher, publication date, format and page count.
Title and authors first generate candidate pairs. The other attributes then decide whether two candidates are the same edition.
We evaluate against the ISBN gold standard.


\section{Phase I: Target Schema and Mappings}

In Phase I, we transform the three book datasets (Goodreads,
DBpedia, and Amazon) into a common XML structure. Based on the
integrated schema presented in Table~\ref{tab:schema}, we created
an XML Schema Definition (\texttt{target.xsd}). As explained
earlier, each target record represents a specific book edition
rather than a work in general.

For each dataset, we create a separate mapping in Altova MapForce
(\texttt{.mfd}), which assigns the source attributes to the
target schema and generates an XML output file.

We followed the same procedure for all three datasets. First,
we analyzed the raw data with Python in the notebook
\texttt{WDI\_Vorab\_Analyse.ipynb} to identify and quantify
different formats, missing values, invalid entries, and other
data quality issues. We then implemented the mappings in
MapForce, making sure that individual invalid values do not
cause the entire transformation to fail.


\subsection{Target Schema and General Mapping Rules}

The target schema contains the 14 descriptive attributes listed
in Table~\ref{tab:schema}. In addition, it includes an
\texttt{isbn} field, which is used only for overlap estimation
and the gold standard, not as a matching attribute. Each book
also receives a unique ID consisting of a source prefix and a
sequential number, such as \texttt{goodreads\_1}.

The XSD defines the data types of the attributes.
\texttt{authors}, \texttt{genres}, and \texttt{awards} are lists
with repeated child elements (\texttt{author}, \texttt{genre},
and \texttt{award}). The publication date uses
\texttt{xs:date}, page count and rating count are integers,
and rating and price are decimal numbers. All remaining
attributes are stored as strings.

All descriptive attributes are optional because not every
source provides all information and invalid values may be
omitted during mapping. The ISBN is stored as a string because
it may start with zero or end with the letter X. In an initial
schema generated from a sample XML file, ISBN was incorrectly
recognized as a number, which caused mapping failures.

\subsubsection{General Mapping Rules}

We import the Goodreads and Amazon CSV files into MapForce
with all fields treated as strings, while DBpedia is imported
directly as XML. Each CSV row (\texttt{Rows}) or DBpedia
\texttt{book} element is mapped to one \texttt{book} element
in the target schema. For list attributes, we connect the
values to the repeated child elements rather than to the
list container.

For numeric values and dates, we use custom functions because
MapForce may stop the entire transformation if a value cannot
be converted, for example the invalid price
\texttt{1.189.88}. Our functions first use
\texttt{match-pattern} (a regular expression) to check
whether a value has a valid format. If several formats are
possible, we select the appropriate conversion using
\texttt{if-else} conditions.

Invalid values are not written to the output, so the
corresponding optional XML element is omitted. We only correct
values when their meaning is clear, for example converting
\texttt{1 page} to \texttt{1}. Otherwise, we leave them out
rather than introducing potentially incorrect information.


\subsection{Goodreads Mapping}

\subsubsection{Data Analysis}

The Goodreads dataset contains 52,478 books. Using Python,
we examined the data structure, date formats, numeric fields,
ISBNs, lists, missing values, and special characters.

Publication dates vary considerably: 42,913 entries (81.8\%)
contain a complete date, 4,362 (8.3\%) contain only a year,
1,219 (2.3\%) already contain January 1 as a complete date,
and 880 (1.7\%) are empty. Another 3,104 entries (5.9\%)
do not match any recognized format. These include incomplete
dates such as \texttt{December 2002} and six cases where
list names appear instead of dates, such as
\texttt{Great Erotica}, probably due to scraping errors.

We also found problems in numeric fields. The \texttt{pages}
column contains \texttt{1 page} instead of a number in
23 cases. Some prices have invalid formats, such as
\texttt{1.189.88}. ISBN problems include placeholders
(\texttt{9999999999999}), values damaged by Excel
(\texttt{9.78044E+12}), and ASINs
(\texttt{B0064CPN7I}), which are not ISBNs.
Genres and awards are stored as Python-style lists within
single CSV fields, for example \texttt{['A', 'B']}.

\subsubsection{Implementation in MapForce}

For publication dates, we created the custom function
\texttt{Parse\_Date}. It recognizes formats such as
\texttt{MM/DD/YY}, \texttt{MM/DD/YYYY}, year-only values,
and dates with written month names and ordinal suffixes,
such as \texttt{April 27th 2010}. All recognized values
are converted to \texttt{YYYY-MM-DD}.

Two-digit years up to 26 are interpreted as 20xx, while
larger values are interpreted as 19xx. Ordinal suffixes
such as \texttt{st}, \texttt{nd}, \texttt{rd}, and
\texttt{th} are removed before conversion because
\texttt{parse-date} cannot process them directly.
For year-only values, we append \texttt{-01-01} because
\texttt{xs:date} requires a complete date. Unrecognized
values are omitted.

For prices, ratings, and rating counts, we use
\texttt{Safe\_Number}, which converts only valid numeric
values. The separate function \texttt{Page\_Count}
also handles entries such as \texttt{1 page} and converts
them to \texttt{1}.

Genres and awards are processed with \texttt{Clean\_List}.
This function splits the values at the separator
\texttt{', '} and creates individual XML child elements.

Before mapping, we cleaned the ISBNs using Python.
Placeholders, Excel-damaged values, and ASINs were removed,
so only valid ISBN-10 and ISBN-13 values are transferred
to the target schema.


\subsection{DBpedia Mapping}

\subsubsection{Data Analysis}

The DBpedia dataset contains 59,270 books and is already
available in XML format. Nevertheless, we identified
several data quality issues.

For \texttt{release\_date}, 58.0\% of the records contain
only a year, 21.8\% a complete date, 6.8\% a month and year,
and 0.5\% already contain January 1. Another 9.2\% are
empty, while 3.6\% contain unusable values such as
\texttt{--11-01} or \texttt{20072010}.

DBpedia also provides a separate \texttt{year} attribute.
We compared it with \texttt{release\_date} and found that
the years agree in 47,554 out of 47,556 comparable records.
This makes \texttt{year} a suitable fallback in most cases.
For 4,933 books, \texttt{release\_date} is unusable but
\texttt{year} is available, allowing us to recover the
publication year.

ISBN data also contain problems. An ISBN is missing for
39.0\% of the books. We found 28,819 ISBN entries with
nine characters or fewer, such as \texttt{0} or
\texttt{978}. In addition, 28,557 books contain two
ISBN entries.

Missing values are especially common for \texttt{pages}
(38.4\%), \texttt{genres} (36.4\%), \texttt{publisher}
(20.5\%), \texttt{language} (20.4\%), and \texttt{authors}
(9.1\%). Furthermore, 5,446 books contain an ampersand
character (\texttt{\&}).

\subsubsection{Implementation in MapForce}

Since DBpedia already uses XML, the \texttt{authors}
and \texttt{genres} elements can be mapped directly
without splitting text-based lists.

For publication dates, we use an \texttt{if-else}
condition combined with \texttt{match-pattern}.
If \texttt{release\_date} contains a complete date,
we convert it using \texttt{parse-date}. Otherwise,
we use the separate \texttt{year} attribute and append
\texttt{-01-01} to obtain a valid \texttt{xs:date}.
This allows us to recover a date for 4,933 books.

For ISBNs, we remove values with nine characters or
fewer and select the first remaining entry. This
filters out obviously incomplete ISBNs, although
it does not perform full ISBN validation.

Attributes that DBpedia does not provide, such as
\texttt{format}, \texttt{edition}, \texttt{rating},
\texttt{ratings\_count}, \texttt{price},
\texttt{series}, and \texttt{awards}, are omitted
from the target output.


\subsection{Amazon Mapping}

\subsubsection{Data Analysis}

Our Amazon dataset contains 40,000 books. The Python
analysis did not identify any shifted CSV rows.

Publication dates are relatively consistent:
31,012 entries (77.5\%) contain a complete date,
such as \texttt{April 30, 2019}, 7,022 (17.6\%)
already contain January 1, and 1,966 (4.9\%) are
empty. Year-only values do not occur.

For numeric fields, \texttt{pages} contains valid
values for 28,197 books (70.5\%) and is empty
for 11,803 (29.5\%). The price is missing for
4,724 books (11.8\%).

Amazon provides two ISBN columns:
\texttt{isbn13} and \texttt{isbn10}. For 85 books
(0.2\%), \texttt{isbn13} is missing, but
\texttt{isbn10} is available in every case.
There are no books where both fields are empty.
Interestingly, \texttt{isbn10} contains 13-digit
ISBNs for 3,029 books (7.6\%). We also found
six ASINs.

Authors and genres are stored as JSON-like lists,
for example \texttt{["A","B"]}. All 40,000 books
use this format for \texttt{authors}. For
\texttt{genres}, 37,042 books (92.6\%) contain
a list with values, while 2,958 (7.4\%) contain
an empty list \texttt{[]}. Unlike Goodreads,
there is no space after the comma.

Other missing values include \texttt{edition}
(40.1\%), \texttt{format} (8.4\%),
\texttt{publisher} (4.7\%), and
\texttt{language} (1.0\%). In addition,
34,187 books (approximately 85\%) contain
an ampersand, for example in
\texttt{Literature \& Fiction}.

\subsubsection{Implementation in MapForce}

For dates, we created the function
\texttt{Amazon\_Date}. It uses
\texttt{parse-date} with the format pattern
\texttt{[MNn] [D1], [Y0001]}. Before conversion,
\texttt{match-pattern} checks whether the value
matches the expected format to prevent errors.

Authors and genres are processed using
\texttt{Clean\_List\_JSON}. First,
\texttt{substring} removes the outer list
characters. Then, \texttt{tokenize} splits
the remaining content at the separator
\texttt{","}. Empty values are filtered out,
so an empty list \texttt{[]} does not create
a \texttt{genre} element.

As with Goodreads, we use \texttt{Page\_Count}
for page counts and \texttt{Safe\_Number}
for prices, ratings, and rating counts.

For ISBNs, we prefer \texttt{isbn13} and use
\texttt{isbn10} only when \texttt{isbn13} is
missing. This fallback is required for 85 books.
The fact that \texttt{isbn10} sometimes contains
13-digit values is not a problem for the mapping
because ISBNs are stored as strings and used
only as identifiers.

Ampersands are automatically written as
\texttt{\&amp;} in XML. This is the correct
XML representation, not a data error.
An XML parser converts it back to the
original ampersand when reading the file.


\subsection{Cross-Source Issue: Ambiguity of January 1}

A common issue is the addition of \texttt{-01-01}
to year-only publication dates. Since the target
schema uses \texttt{xs:date}, a complete date is
required. For example, \texttt{2010} becomes
\texttt{2010-01-01}.

However, some source records already contain
January 1 as their publication date. In the
resulting XML files, we can no longer distinguish
between dates where January 1 was originally
present and dates where we added it.

We used Python to quantify both cases:

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{January 1 dates in the three datasets.}
\label{tab:january-first}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Dataset} &
\textbf{January 1 added} &
\textbf{January 1 in source} \\
\midrule
Goodreads & 4,362 & 1,219 \\
DBpedia   & 34,355 & 321 \\
Amazon    & 0 & 7,022 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

For Goodreads, the 1,219 original January 1 dates
represent approximately 22\% of all January 1
values after conversion. For DBpedia, only about
0.9\% of these values were originally present
(321 compared with 34,355 added dates).

Amazon is different: we did not need to add
January 1 to any year-only values, but 7,022
books (17.6\%) already have this date.
This unusually high share suggests that Amazon
may use January 1 as a default when the exact
publication day is unknown. However, we cannot
confirm this from the available data.

This ambiguity is relevant for matching in
Phase II because comparing complete dates
could lead to incorrect differences or matches.
We therefore plan to compare only the
publication year.

Another cross-source issue concerns missing
values. A missing value in the target output
may already have been missing in the source,
or it may result from our cleaning process
when an invalid value was deliberately omitted.
We analyzed and quantified these problems
with Python before implementing the mappings
to make our decisions traceable.
